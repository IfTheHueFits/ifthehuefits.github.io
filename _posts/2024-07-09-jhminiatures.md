I've spent over 100 hours painting a single miniature in the past, and yet, in two days I have managed to produce what is, without doubt, the best miniature I have ever painted. All this is because last weekend I had the pleasure of returning to Bad Moon Cafe for a painting class with Juan Hidalgo of JHMiniatures.

<!--more-->

I have been a follower of Juan's for years. As one of the first people to really show me the versatility of contrast paints, I have always loved Juan's work for his straightforward, low-effort, but high-reward painting results. I had also seen intermittent posts on his social media of a pretty cool Mandalorian bust but never quite twigged it was part of a painting class until I just stumbled upon a post that spaces were available for the coming weekend's class in London and for the first weekend in ages, I was completely free.

## What's in the class?

The class itself cost €170 (About £150), and for my money I got:
- A 3D-printed Mandalorian bust
- 2 days (16hrs) of tuition from Juan on how to paint the bust from start to finish
- All paints provided
- An STL of the bust so that we can print our own and keep practising in future
- A video recording of the class to fondly look back on

All in all, I felt this was excellent value for money. The inclusion of the video and STL as well provided a wonderful cherry on top to make sure I could get the most out of the class by being able to practice again in my own time without having to remember every detail. The bust we were provided with was a great quality print - only very minor stepping on the top of the helmet which is now invisible after a couple of layers of paint, as well as a handy holder that the bust was pinned into. The bust also was already zenithal primed so that we could get cracking straight away.

All we had to provide for the class was our own brushes, a suitable lamp, and a wet palette.

## Day 1 - Non-Metallic Metal

The focus of the first day of the class was solely to work on the beskar armour on the bust. This was to be done in Non-metallic metal using a sketching and blending technique. Effectively, start by placing the rough highlights in blocks on the armour, working up through a couple of colours from light to dark (the sketch), and then smooth out the transition between each of the highlight layers with intermediate mixes of colours (the blend) to tidy up the mini.

Juan spent the first 30 minutes showing us how to sort the initial sketches from the base coat up to the top highlight before we could then have a go ourselves. I'll admit, at first, I was initially disappointed that we were only going to be painting the front of the bust in the class. However, it quickly became apparent that we never would have had the time to do the back of the bust so I'm thankful we focussed on the important parts! Not to mention it gives me time to keep practicing and getting the back done in my own time.

After the initial demo, Juan was regularly giving feedback to everyone as they went along, providing great explanations of how to understand where to put highlights as well as feedback on technique. I think perhaps one of the most enlightening moments was when Juan explained that NMM always looks better on camera, I went over with my initial sketch, which I was happy with, but knew it could be better. Juan then showed mine next to his side by side on the video screen and it instantly became apparent what he was talking about - it actually filled me with confidence to know I was along the right lines, as in 2D it made the effect look much more what I was used to seeing from painters on Instagram!

One of the other things I have always struggled with for NMM was knowing precisely where the highlights should be placed to sell the effect without oversaturating the model. Having Juan's demo model passed around as a reference gave us time to really study his work and think about what was necessary and where, as well as where there could be some flexibility to play with the reflections ourselves.

The initial sketch took about 2 hours to complete. Juan then did a quick demo of the blending stages and then we had a break for lunch. I hadn't actually realised there was going to be a break and so, because I had finished slightly early I had already eaten I had decided not to join Juan and the other students for lunch as I was desperate to get on with the blending and give myself as much time to ask questions as possible. I'm glad I did, as it gave me just that bit more time to get into the swing of things and refine my blends how I wanted to, but everyone still had plenty of time to finish the job to a great standard.

The rest of the afternoon was spent blending. It sounds like a long time, but actually, it flew by and by the end of day 1, I was already incredibly happy with my results.

## Day 2 - OSL and the rest of the mini

So the hard bit was out of the way, and on day 2 we had the simple task of just finishing the rest of the bust!

We started with the fun part that would really make the mini pop - a warm OSL glow to contrast the cool beskar. Rather than painting the OSL in the same sketch and blend manner that we were taught the previous day, Juan mixed things up a bit by instead showing us how to glaze the OSL in and make the most of the orange paint's translucency. This was a really great variation to give us plenty of practice with lots of different brush techniques and improve our overall brush control.

I was blown away by how straightforward it was to make such an impactful addition to the model. Even the initial dark orange layer added a real atmosphere to the bust. By the time we had glazed over the top yellow highlights, I couldn't pull my eyes away from what I had somehow managed to achieve. Again, the entire OSL process took 2-ish hours from start to finish, including Juan's demonstrations.

The rest of the afternoon was then spent painting all the remaining materials. Most of the schemes were incredibly simple, starting with some kind of basecoat and a top highlight colour, we just progressively added more and more of the highlight colour until we were happy. These stages really showed how the wet palette comes into its own as a real powerhouse for mini painting: it made getting lots of intermediate shades while being able to go back and forth to tidy so easy, and it allowed me to take a step back from worrying about matching every colour with what I had done previously and instead embrace the slight variations to make the materials look more authentic. In addition, it meant I didn't feel limited to a discrete set of tones but could add intermediate colours that happenned to appear where I had mixed the two colours on my palette.  Juan was also very clear in justifying the specific paints we were using. In particular, we always came back to including the same initial base colour in our mixes to tie all the materials together, as well as playing around with temperature as well as value to improve the visual appeal and contrast on the mini.

## The Venue

As mentioned above, the class was held at Bad Moon Cafe. It's a great venue and I had thoroughly enjoyed my previous class there with Mamikon, so I was glad to be back. They also had a great selection of snacks, drinks and food to keep us fuelled for the full two days. My only minor gripe was that it was incredibly busy on Day 2, to the point where Juan was struggling at times to be heard over the other groups in the venue. However, that's not really anyone's fault and it was great to see so many people hobbying in so many different ways.

Since Juan was also filming the class for our future recording, he also had a second screen set up. This was invaluable so we didn't all need to be crowded behind him to see what was going on and could still have a clear view of everything from our seats. All in all the presentation and running of the class were super slick and very professional.

## Final thoughts

The bust is currently sitting on my desk as I write this. Every couple of minutes I keep having to stop, pick it up and stare in disbelief that I was the one to paint it. The back is not yet finished, but I certainly want to pick up a copy of each of the paints myself to finish it off. I'm also tempted to print another to try the same scheme but change where the light is coming from to see if I can master the highlight placement without the safety net of Juan's demo.

At lunch on the second day, Juan told us about his plans for future classes. Suffice it to say I'm sold, and as soon as tickets go up for the next one (which should be on a new bust too!) I will be instantly booking myself on. I also thoroughly enjoyed the variety of painting a bust as opposed to a 28mm mini. The increased scale was much more forgiving as a scratch or bad blend was a smaller proportion of the overall surface so helped sell the look rather than shatter the illusion.

The entire class was taught using the new Vallejo Model Colour paints. They are a great range, and much more matte than Citadel so may not be for everyone's tastes. It would have been nice if there was some sort of bundle deal alongside the class, either from Juan or Bad Moon, to buy all the paints used in some kind of multi-buy discount - it feels like it would have been a win-win for the venue and Juan, but again, a very minor nitpick given we had all the paints we needed provided to us anyway.

There are still a few more classes on the Mandalorian bust by Juan across Europe over the next couple of months. A few have sold out, but if you get a chance to attend any of the others that have space left, being taught by Juan is not an opportunity you want to pass up.
